I Was Just Bored

It started with me being bored. Not productively bored — just lying on my phone, scrolling through nothing in particular. I ended up on this activity site that gives you random things to do when you have nothing to do. Good concept. I spent about ten minutes reading through the suggestions.

I did not do any of them.

Instead, I thought: this site is cool, I should make something like it. And then — before I even finished the thought — I started doing exactly that. From my phone. No laptop, no VS Code, no terminal. Just Claude, Safari, and the notes app.

The Constraint Was the Point

I decided to keep it simple: one HTML file, AI-generated, ship it from the phone. The whole thing had to be done without touching a keyboard. That's the constraint I set myself, and honestly it's what made it interesting.

I opened Claude and started prompting. Something like: "Build me a single-page website that gives people random activities to do when they're bored. Make it look clean and modern."

What came back was... pretty solid actually. A full HTML page, inline CSS, some JavaScript for the randomize button. I copied it into a raw HTML preview tool and it worked first try.

That's when vibe coding clicks for me — that moment where you describe what you want, and something real comes out the other side almost immediately.

The Friction Was Real Though

Let's not romanticise it too much. Coding from a phone is annoying in ways you forget until you're doing it. Fat-finger typos in prompts. Scrolling through 200 lines of HTML with your thumb trying to find where the bug is. Copy-pasting code between apps is a small nightmare. The autocorrect changing "div" to "dive" in my prompt.

But there's something weirdly satisfying about working through those constraints. Every little thing that works feels like a win because it was slightly harder to get there.

I iterated maybe four or five times. Tweaking the layout, adjusting the colour scheme, asking Claude to add a category filter. Each iteration was just: describe what I want to change, copy the new code, preview it. No build process, no npm install, no waiting. Just a file.

The Result

Looked fine. Actually looked good. If you showed it to someone without context, they wouldn't guess it was made in an hour on a phone. That's the thing about vibe coding — the floor is so low now that the gap between "idea" and "something that looks real" is basically nothing.

I dropped it on a static host, shared the link in a group chat. People thought it was a proper project.

The Part Worth Thinking About

Here's where I want to be honest though, because I think the hype around vibe coding glosses over this.

For personal stuff? It's genuinely great. Low stakes, quick iteration, you learn what you like, you ship something you're proud of. There's no downside. The phone experiment was fun and I'll do it again.

But when people start treating it like a shortcut to making money — that's where I'd slow down. The same thing that makes it feel effortless is the same thing that makes it brittle. You don't fully understand what you're shipping. The AI writes code you can't always debug. Security, scalability, edge cases — these things don't disappear just because Claude didn't mention them.

The honest take Vibe coding is great for personal projects. If you want to make money from it, be more careful. When something seems too good to be true, it usually is.

I'm not saying don't do it. I'm saying know what you're getting. Use it to explore, to prototype, to scratch your own itch. Just don't hand the keys of a production system to code you don't understand because it looked impressive coming out of Claude.

Anyway. Next time you're bored, you don't even need a laptop.
